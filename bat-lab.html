How to read this

Swing weight is the real currency, not weight. Two 33/30 bats can differ by 600 oz·in² in moment of inertia depending on where the wood sits. That is why a 271 and a C243 stamped with the same number feel nothing alike. Weight is what the scale says; swing weight is what your hands say.

Profile moves swing weight less than size does. Across the wood profiles at a fixed length and weight, the spread is usually 6–9%. One inch and one ounce is typically 12–15%. So switching from a C243 to a 271 is a real change, but it is roughly half of what going up a size does — and in the opposite direction.

Certification is a separate lever entirely. BBCOR, USA Baseball, and wood are all capped at the same 0.50 performance ceiling on purpose. USSSA bats are not, which is why a USSSA bat outhits a USA bat of identical dimensions by a margin no shape change can touch. If you are comparing across certifications, that term usually dominates everything else.

Distance is not the whole decision. The bar chart shows what each change costs in the time it takes to get the barrel to the ball. A few milliseconds sounds like nothing, but a pitch travels about 4 inches in 3 ms, and that is roughly the difference between fair and foul. Most hitters are better off a size under their maximum-distance bat.

The collision itself is exit = q·v_pitch + [(1+e)/(1+r)]·v_bat, with r = ball mass / effective mass at contact and e set by the certification. Effective mass comes from the bat's own geometry: 1/M_eff = 1/M + d²/I_cm, where d is the distance from the balance point to where you hit it.

Bats are modeled as solids of revolution (wood) or thin shells (metal) built from published profile dimensions — handle diameter, taper length, barrel length and diameter, knob, end cup, end load — then scaled to the selected weight, exactly as a manufacturer picks billet density or wall thickness. Moments of inertia are integrated from that geometry, not looked up, so absolute swing weights land within a few percent of published values rather than exactly on them. Differences between bats are far more trustworthy than the absolute numbers. Bat speed vs. swing weight uses a force-velocity curve anchored to your Bat A speed. Trajectories include drag and Magnus lift, calibrated so 100 mph at 28° carries about 395 ft.
